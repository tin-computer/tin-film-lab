# Start with something that happens

Use these as private prompts for the agent, not a menu to send to every customer. Read the product first. Offer two ideas that fit its action, audience and available material, with a short reason for the recommendation. Offer a third only when it adds a different story. Accept a free answer. Fit these alternatives into the four-question onboarding in FILM_FLOW.md. An answered or delegated brief needs no extra question.

Changing a heading does not make a familiar template original. Describe who does what, what changes and why the viewer cares. Write customer-facing options in their language. Do not show labels such as “Gen Z”, “cracked founder”, archive IDs or production terms as a personality test.

## Keep the requested structure

A user can ask for a familiar structure, a combination or a new idea. Keep that choice in plain language; matching it to a pool name is optional. Do not add humour, characters or a physical metaphor merely to make a direct demonstration look different.

| Requested structure | A possible starting point |
| --- | --- |
| Problem to solution | Show the obstacle, then the supported product action and its useful result |
| Before and after | Repeat the same task with a meaningful change; “Try that again” can help |
| Direct feature demonstration | Follow the input, product action and output; “Follow it all the way” can help |
| Result first | Open on the result, then show its cause; “Start with the strange result” can help |

Choose a filming approach that helps that particular story. Save the actual events and treatment in `BRIEF.md`, carry them into `STORY.md` and review the rendered result through [FILM_FLOW.md](FILM_FLOW.md). The pool is not a limit on which stories can be made.

## Story moves

| Working name | What happens | Where it earns its place |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Follow it all the way | Stay with one message, file or sentence as the product changes it and someone uses the result | A workflow whose handoffs are usually invisible |
| Try that again | The same person attempts the same job twice; one specific obstacle changes | A product with a clear, supported before/after difference |
| Start with the strange result | Open on an outcome, then reveal the small action that caused it | An outcome that can be understood before the mechanism |
| A bet between friends | A small challenge exposes what actually matters; the product makes the result visible | Competition, learning or collaboration with a real result |
| One button, a control room | Treat a tiny task like a huge operation; a simple product action ends the fuss | Builder humour and over-engineering, when the task is recognisable |
| The plan lasts three seconds | Someone confidently starts; a familiar detail interrupts them; they adapt with the product | Internet humour based on an observed user frustration, not random memes |
| Say it with a straight face | A calm narrator or character treats an ordinary habit as an extraordinary discovery | A dry documentary or an absurdly serious explanation |
| The room obeys | A real product action changes a physical object or space; return to the actual product result | A physical metaphor that explains the feature rather than replacing it |

The last four include the earlier over-engineering, internet-humour, mock-documentary and broken-physics ideas. They are available to any audience whose tone fits; age and “founder” labels are not evidence of taste. Do not invent capabilities, customer results or measurable savings to make a joke work.

## Ways to shoot it

Mix these with a story; they are not fixed story/style packages. Use only the choices that the available tools and material can support.

| Working name | Picture and movement | Sound and pacing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stay beside the person | Close, readable details and the person's reaction; the product remains part of the action | Room sound, a short line or restrained music; space to notice the result |
| One object takes us there | Follow a shared shape or object across cuts, with a visible cause for each change | A small repeated sound connects scenes; save the strongest hit for the result |
| Hold it, then let it go | A tight or still composition builds pressure; the frame opens after the decisive action | Sparse opening, a pause if useful, then release; no arbitrary drop |
| Let the material do the moving | Paper, print, light, shadow or a tangible surface changes with the feature | Contact sounds and selective rhythm; texture carries an action, not decoration |
| Keep a straight face | A patient wide shot or an overly formal close-up makes the absurd situation readable | Dry narration, ordinary room sound or a deliberate silence; no laugh cue required |
| The screen spills into the room | A screen action continues in physical space, then lands back in a clear product state | Carry the same sound through the handoff so the cause is easy to follow |

Use music, effects and narration as parts of this scene. A waveform helps place an event; it cannot decide whether the emotion fits. Read the owner's listening notes and exclusions. Do not reuse another owner's preferences as defaults. Keep essential product text and chosen speech captions; remove decorative eyebrows, filler headings and an unrequested CTA.

## Cinematic examples to adapt

Consider these when the product has a clear obstacle, a useful surprise or a funny difference between the size of a task and how seriously people treat it. Offer one when it fits, or use it when the user asks. Keep the user's preferred tone; a serious product does not need a joke. These examples extend the pool, not a fixed menu or the default for every film.

| Approach | What could happen | Picture and sound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A movie trailer for a tiny task | An agent finishes its work, but its owner has left the desk. Treat this small obstacle like a major film event, then reveal the useful phone message and the owner's reply. | Serious framing and a restrained opening build to a strong music hit when the actual product action appears. End on a short human exchange or useful result, not a list of claims. |
| Wait for the reply | Follow a person away from their computer while a familiar task stays unresolved. Hold on the question; the product delivers the answer and we see what the person can do next. | Close details, a held shot and sparse sound create anticipation. A notification or other relevant practical sound introduces the answer; leave time to understand it. Do not invent a crisis, countdown or instant-result claim. |
| The music stops for something ordinary | Build a grand expectation around a real user problem. Cut the music just before a small, helpful product action resolves it; let the ordinary reply provide the surprise. | A musical build gives way to silence or room sound. The product action and its effect remain readable. Use a final hit only if it serves the ending; no compulsory drop, logo card or repeated punchline. |

For the messaging example, a customer-facing suggestion could be: “Let's make leaving your agent at the desk feel like a movie trailer. As the music builds, your phone gets a simple ‘Ready to ship?’ message; you reply and the work continues.” Offer a different direction beside it when the brief is open. A viewer should understand the idea without knowing our Claw film.

Keep the familiar genre useful without copying a reference film's shots, dialogue, music or branding. Do not copy the old “IN A WORLD” cards or turn cinematic pacing into a wall of text. The opening can be a visual action, sound or reaction. Adapt the events and music to the agreed duration and aspect ratio; do not inherit the Claw trailer's 30 seconds, music, lettering or ending. Save the chosen variation in the brief and check the product action and result in the actual preview.

## What the customer should actually see

Instead of “Problem–solution, cinematic, Gen Z”, offer two concrete alternatives for the current product. For example, if the product evidence supports code-to-translation files:

- “We follow one button label from code, into a translation key, then into the same product screen in another language.”
- “A team prepares a huge control room to translate one button. A developer runs the command; we end on the working translated screen.”

Explain which is clearer for this viewer and why. If the owner wants to go further, change an event, viewpoint or rule of the scene. Do not merely add a new background, font, slang or a louder track. Ask about boldness only when that missing answer changes the direction; “keep it grounded”, “push it further” and a free answer are enough.

Before rendering, check: can someone name the product action and its consequence from this story? After rendering, check the actual first seconds and result, including a phone-size view. This direction guide is not proof that the film is clear, funny or effective; keep those review results separate from technical checks.
